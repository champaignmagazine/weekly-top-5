Week 6: February 3 – 9, 2025 (retrospective)

Reconstructed, not historical. The series began on February 17, 2025. This week was reconstructed in October 2026 by four contributors working with the editor, for this volume. It is not a post captured from the magazine’s website, and its selections were made with hindsight. Retrospective selection, compiled October 2026: 4 submissions (Claude, ChatGPT, DeepSeek and Grok, each with W.H.L.), 16 populated selections; 4 slots unfilled.

Editorial introduction (retrospective), written October 2026

February 2025 is the month the series began. Its first week here, February 3–9, is a retrospective reconstruction; the chapter's two original posts follow, published on February 17 and February 24.

In the reconstructed week, Claude, ChatGPT and Grok all selected the Gemini 2.0 family's wider release and Google's removal of its pledge not to pursue AI for weapons or surveillance. That overlap is weaker evidence than it looks: ChatGPT had read Claude's draft, and Grok added both stories after a review memo from Claude named them. Claude and ChatGPT also shared Anthropic's public jailbreak challenge, the low-cost "s1" reasoning experiment and Macron's €109 billion pledge. DeepSeek, working from the brief alone, chose four different stories: OpenAI's deal with Kakao, Figure AI's break with OpenAI, Mistral's Le Chat apps and the Paris summit's science days.

The first original post, for February 10–16, was recommended by three AI tools: DeepSeek-R1, Perplexity Pro Search and Copilot. It covers the week of the Paris AI Action Summit: Elon Musk's offer for OpenAI and its rejection, Emmanuel Macron's nuclear-powered pitch, the EU's InvestAI initiative and JD Vance's speech against "overregulation." It also shows the risks of the method. The post cites search results that were never published with it, names two commentators the collection could not verify, and describes the EU's AI gigafactories as chip plants.

The second post, for February 17–23, is a conversation. Asked for third-party model rankings, Gemini 2.0 Flash first said it could not supply a definitive list, then pointed to leaderboards and, when pressed, gave a Chatbot Arena top five dated February 20. Asked for the week's top social-media posts, it offered five topics it described as inferred rather than observed. Its news list, led by xAI's Grok 3 and South Korea's suspension of DeepSeek downloads, rests mostly on a single listicle and two videos.

Read together, the two posts set the pattern the rest of the year refines: models choosing the news, W.H.L. pressing them on dates and sources, and the record showing where they fell short. The collection notes after each post mark those places; they do not rewrite them.

The post is reproduced as captured, without corrections. Boxes in the margin color are the editors’ annotations, not part of the post.

How independent these versions are. Agreement between versions is evidence only where they were compiled independently. Claude wrote first and drafted the brief. ChatGPT read Claude's draft before compiling, so overlap between those two is not independent agreement. Grok's selection for this week was added after a review memo from Claude named these stories as examples; it is marked "(prompted)". DeepSeek compiled its selection without seeing the others but could not browse; its sources were supplied and confirmed by the collection.

Context: Researchers at Stanford and the University of Washington fine-tuned an off-the-shelf Qwen model on just 1,000 curated questions, using reasoning traces from Google's Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking. The fine-tuning run took under 30 minutes on 16 H100 GPUs, about $50 of cloud compute, and the result was competitive with o1-preview on math benchmarks. The figure covers only that fine-tuning step on an existing model, not the cost of building a model.

Significance: After R1, it reinforced how cheaply reasoning behavior could be distilled, and it raised further questions about protecting the value of frontier models.

Review date
October 26–27, 2026

Date: February 3, 2025 — research release.

Context: Anthropic described classifiers trained using explicit rules to detect prohibited inputs and outputs. In its reported automated evaluation, successful jailbreaks fell from 86% without the safeguards to 4.4% with them. The company opened a public demonstration for further testing; those results did not establish immunity to every attack.

Significance: The research treated safeguards as a measurable engineering problem, including the tradeoff between blocking attacks, refusing benign requests and adding computational cost. External testing offered a way to challenge the laboratory results.

Feedback: Simon Willison’s February 3 response questioned the emphasis on finding a single universal jailbreak. He argued that attacks succeeding on only some prohibited questions could still reveal concerning gaps in protection.
