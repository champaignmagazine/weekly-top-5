Introduction

February 2025

Major events

February was the month of the Paris AI Action Summit, and of the competing ideas of AI it put on one stage. France pitched its nuclear-powered electricity and announced €109 billion in prospective private investment; the European Commission launched InvestAI to mobilize €200 billion, including funds for AI “gigafactories”; European companies formed an AI Champions initiative. US Vice President JD Vance used the same summit to argue against heavy regulation. Days earlier, Google had dropped its pledge not to apply AI to weapons or surveillance.

The companies kept shipping. Google made Gemini 2.0 Flash generally available, Mistral took Le Chat to phones, Adobe and YouTube put generated video into creative tools, and xAI unveiled Grok 3 with reasoning models. Control was contested too: OpenAI’s board rejected a $97.4 billion offer from an Elon Musk-led consortium, publishers sued Cohere over their content, and South Korea’s privacy regulator saw DeepSeek’s app pulled from local stores while it addressed the country’s data law.

Patterns and trends

Two approaches to governing AI stood side by side: Europe paired large investment programs with the AI Act, whose first obligations had applied on February 2, while Washington argued that regulation would hold the industry back. Companies, meanwhile, moved to own more of what they depended on. OpenAI was reported to be finishing its first chip design, and Figure AI left its collaboration with OpenAI to build its own robot models. Research looked for cheaper routes to better models: Anthropic’s Constitutional Classifiers resisted jailbreaks in a public challenge, and the s1 experiment showed what a 1,000-example fine-tune plus extra thinking time could do.

Long-term threads

Europe’s program continued with the AI Continent Action Plan in April, the Code of Practice for general-purpose models in July and the obligations for those models in August. OpenAI’s chip work resurfaced in September reports of a chip built with Broadcom and in October’s 10-gigawatt agreement with the company. The Musk dispute moved to court with OpenAI’s countersuit in April. The Cohere case was an early sign of the copyright disputes that ran through the year, from June’s fair-use rulings on generative-AI training to Anthropic’s settlement in September. And DeepSeek’s trouble with privacy regulators, begun in Italy in January, now reached South Korea.

P-2025-W06-01

Constitutional Classifiers face adversarial testing

February 3

Anthropic introduced input and output classifiers trained with synthetic examples derived from written rules. The company reported stronger resistance to jailbreaks in its evaluations and opened a public challenge. Its earlier prototype had also imposed substantial computing overhead and rejected too many harmless requests.1

The work treated safety as a system-level engineering problem, with robustness, cost and ordinary usability measured together.

s1 explores reasoning with a small training set

February 3 revision; first posted January 31

The s1 researchers fine-tuned Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on 1,000 questions paired with reasoning traces. Their “budget forcing” method controlled how long the model continued reasoning. The paper reported improvements on specified mathematics benchmarks and released the model, data and code.2

The experiment separated two ways to improve answers: fine-tuning an existing model and allocating more computation when it answers.

Google revises its AI principles

February 4

Google replaced its earlier framework with principles centered on innovation, responsible development and collaboration. The revised principles omitted the 2018 exclusions concerning weapons and surveillance that violated internationally accepted norms. Google linked the revision to global competition and the national-security interests of democracies.3,4,5

The change altered the company’s stated boundaries for acceptable applications of AI.

Kakao and OpenAI announce a partnership

February 4

Kakao announced plans to incorporate OpenAI technology into its Korean services, including Kanana and its wider digital ecosystem. The companies also planned joint product development, while Kakao intended to deploy ChatGPT Enterprise internally.6

The partnership connected model development to an established distribution network, with the integrations still to be built.

P-2025-W06-05

Figure ends its collaboration with OpenAI

February 4

Humanoid-robot maker Figure AI said it was leaving its agreement with OpenAI after a “major breakthrough” in robot AI built in-house. Chief executive Brett Adcock said that “to solve embodied AI at scale in the real world, you have to vertically integrate robot AI,” and promised to show something new within 30 days.7

The split showed robotics companies seeking to own the models that drive their machines, a year after the two companies’ partnership was announced.

Gemini 2.0 expands for developers

February 5

Google made Gemini 2.0 Flash generally available through the Gemini API, Google AI Studio and Vertex AI. Flash-Lite entered public preview, while Gemini 2.0 Pro remained experimental. Flash supported multimodal input and text output; other output modes were still planned.8

The release offered developers a choice of cost and capability, with different readiness levels across the family.

Science opens the Paris summit program

February 6–7

The Paris AI Action Week program placed an international conference on AI, Science and Society at the Institut Polytechnique de Paris before the leaders’ summit. Its agenda addressed AI’s effects on scientific research and society, bringing an academic strand into the wider policy gathering.10

The program connected research questions with the decisions facing governments and industry.

France links AI investment to electricity supply

February 10

At the Paris summit, Macron presented France’s nuclear-based electricity supply, available sites and engineering talent as advantages for AI investment. He urged companies to build computing facilities in France and called for faster development.12

His pitch brought the physical requirements of AI into view: power, land and construction capacity, alongside software and research.

European companies launch AI Champions

February 10

General Catalyst launched the EU AI Champions Initiative with more than 60 European companies. The initiative aimed to connect technology providers, investors and established businesses to accelerate AI adoption across industry.13

Its emphasis was practical deployment: linking model capabilities to manufacturing, energy and other sectors with existing customers and operations.

OpenAI is reported to be finishing its first chip design

Reported February 10

Reuters reported that OpenAI was finalizing the design of its first in-house AI chip, to be made by TSMC on its 3-nanometer process and developed with Broadcom. The design was expected to go to fabrication within months, with mass production targeted for 2026. OpenAI and TSMC declined to comment.14

The effort aimed to reduce OpenAI’s dependence on Nvidia for the hardware its models run on.

InvestAI targets €200 billion in investment

February 11

The European Commission launched InvestAI to mobilize €200 billion for AI, including a proposed €20 billion European fund for AI gigafactories: planned large computing facilities for training advanced models. The initiative combined public and private financing; €200 billion was a mobilization target, not EU budget spending.15

The proposal sought to widen access to the computing capacity needed for large-scale model development.

Vance argues for lighter AI regulation

February 11

US Vice President JD Vance used his Paris summit speech to advocate policies favoring AI investment and development. He warned that excessive regulation could inhibit the industry and stressed the importance of electricity, semiconductors and American technological leadership.16

The speech made a clear policy case for prioritizing expansion and infrastructure in international AI discussions.

Adobe presented the model as commercially safe, bringing generated footage into a creative workflow linked to its editing applications.

February 14

xAI introduces Grok 3

February 17, Pacific time

xAI unveiled Grok 3 on the evening of February 17 in the United States. Its subsequent technical announcement described Grok 3 and Grok 3 mini reasoning models, using additional computation to work through problems before answering.21,22

The release extended competition in reasoning systems; the benchmark results published with it were xAI’s own evaluations.

DeepSeek pauses new downloads in South Korea

Announced February 17; effective February 15

South Korea’s privacy regulator announced that DeepSeek had removed its app from local app stores while addressing compliance with personal-data law. New downloads stopped on February 15. Existing app users and web users could still access the service; the English notice appeared on February 18.23

The episode showed how data-handling requirements could affect the distribution of an AI service after launch.

P-2025-W08-03

Multi-Novelty tests more varied model outputs

February 18

The Multi-Novelty preprint proposed supplying multiple textual and visual perspectives to models during inference. Experiments included GPT-4o and other language models, with responses assessed for diversity, novelty and correctness. The authors reported improvements under their chosen evaluation methods.24

The work explored variation in outputs without changing model architecture.
