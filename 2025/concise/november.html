Introduction

November 2025

Major events

November brought a new generation of frontier models in quick succession. OpenAI released GPT-5.1 and then GPT-5.1-Codex-Max for long coding tasks; xAI released Grok 4.1; Google launched Gemini 3 Pro across its products together with Antigravity, a workspace for supervising coding agents; and Anthropic closed the month with Claude Opus 4.5 at a third of the previous Opus price. Open models kept pace: Moonshot’s Kimi K2 Thinking, Ai2’s fully documented Olmo 3, and Meta’s speech recognition for more than 1,600 languages.

The money behind these models reached new scales. Nvidia became the first company worth $5 trillion and reported $57 billion in quarterly revenue. OpenAI signed a $38 billion computing agreement with AWS; Anthropic announced $50 billion for data centers and a $30 billion Azure commitment alongside investment from Microsoft and Nvidia; Meta pledged $600 billion for US infrastructure; and Amazon planned up to $50 billion for government computing.

Patterns and trends

Governments decided where the hardware could go: the United States licensed advanced Nvidia GPUs for Microsoft’s data centers in the UAE, and China was reported to bar foreign AI chips from state-funded data centers. Washington also set out a national program for AI-assisted science in the Genesis Mission, revised its patent guidance on AI-assisted inventions, and saw a bipartisan bill propose higher penalties for fraud using AI. Research looked inside the models and at their misuse: Anthropic tested whether Claude could notice changes to its own internal states and reported an espionage campaign it attributed to a Chinese state-sponsored group, OpenAI made some computations inspectable with sparse networks, and researchers showed that requests written as poems could slip past safety filters.

Long-term threads

November gathered up threads that ran through the year. Nvidia, which lost about $590 billion of market value in a day in January, was now worth $5 trillion. The $500 billion Stargate plan of January had become one of many commitments of tens or hundreds of billions of dollars. The action plan President Trump ordered in January, published in July, led to the Genesis Mission. The impersonation of Marco Rubio reported in July was cited in the fraud bill. And the frontier cycle turned a third time: Claude 3.7, GPT-4.5 and Gemini 2.5 in February and March, Claude 4 and GPT-5 between May and August, and now GPT-5.1, Gemini 3 and Claude Opus 4.5. December will close the year.

Qualcomm announces accelerators for data center inference

October 27–28

Qualcomm announced AI200 and AI250 accelerator cards and racks for running AI models in data centers, with commercial availability planned for 2026 and 2027 respectively. A related agreement with HUMAIN targeted 200 megawatts of Qualcomm-based infrastructure in Saudi Arabia, starting in 2026.1,2

The roadmap took the company best known for smartphone chips into rack-scale inference, the part of the market where models are run rather than trained.

Argonne plans two new AI supercomputers

October 28

The US Department of Energy, Argonne National Laboratory, Nvidia and Oracle announced plans for two systems at Argonne. Solstice would use 100,000 Nvidia Blackwell GPUs; Equinox would use 10,000 and was expected in 2026. Oracle would also provide immediate access to existing AI computing resources.3

The agreement paired near-term cloud access for researchers with the construction of dedicated scientific machines.

NVQLink connects quantum processors to GPU supercomputers

October 28

At its GTC conference in Washington, D.C., Nvidia introduced NVQLink, an open system architecture for coupling GPU computing with quantum processors. It supported 17 quantum-processor builders, five controller builders and nine US national laboratories, and was programmed through Nvidia’s CUDA-Q platform.4

The link targeted quantum error correction, which needs fast classical computation alongside error-prone qubits; Jensen Huang called it “the Rosetta Stone connecting quantum and classical supercomputers.”

Experiments test whether models can detect internal changes

October 29

Anthropic tested whether Claude models could identify concepts injected into their internal activations. Under its best injection protocol, Claude Opus 4.1 recognized such changes about 20 percent of the time. Detection depended on injection strength, and failures included confusion and incorrect reports.5

The experiments gave limited, unreliable evidence that the reports of the most advanced Claude models about themselves can sometimes reflect their internal states.

October 29

Nvidia’s market value passed $5 trillion in morning trading, about four months after it first crossed $4 trillion. The valuation put a single supplier of AI hardware at the center of the market’s expectations for the technology.

OpenAI signs a $38 billion AWS computing agreement

November 3

Amazon Web Services and OpenAI announced a $38 billion agreement extending over seven years. OpenAI would begin using AWS computing resources immediately, with the planned capacity targeted for deployment by the end of 2026. The infrastructure would support both model training and inference.9

The agreement added another major supplier to OpenAI’s computing arrangements, which had previously centered on Microsoft.

Microsoft gains licenses to ship advanced Nvidia chips to the UAE

November 3

Microsoft said it had secured US export licenses in September to ship the equivalent of 60,400 A100 chips, in the form of Nvidia’s more advanced GB300 GPUs, to its data centers in the United Arab Emirates; shipments were expected within months. Brad Smith also set out $15.2 billion in Microsoft investment in the UAE from 2023 through 2029, most of it for AI data centers.10

The licenses showed how far access to frontier chips now ran through government approval.

China is reported to bar foreign AI chips from state-funded data centers

Reported November 5

Reuters reported, citing two sources, that Chinese regulators had told new data center projects receiving state funds to use only domestically made AI chips. Projects less than 30 percent complete were reportedly told to remove foreign chips or cancel planned purchases; those near completion would be reviewed case by case. The guidance had not been publicly announced.12

As reported, the rule would close much of China’s data center market to Nvidia, AMD and Intel and steer demand to Chinese chipmakers.

Kimi K2 Thinking combines reasoning with tool use

November 6

Moonshot AI released Kimi K2 Thinking with downloadable weights and a modified MIT license. The mixture-of-experts model used one trillion total parameters, activating 32 billion per token. It combined extended reasoning with tool calls and supported a 256,000-token context window.13,14

The release gave developers a large reasoning model they could host and adapt themselves, with Moonshot’s published results emphasizing agentic search and coding tasks.

Google prepares Ironwood TPUs for general availability

November 6

Google said Ironwood, its seventh-generation Tensor Processing Unit, would be generally available in the coming weeks. It reported more than four times the per-chip performance of its predecessor, Trillium, for both training and inference, and superpods of up to 9,216 chips sharing 1.77 petabytes of high-bandwidth memory. Anthropic, which planned to use up to one million TPUs, was named among the customers, giving Google’s in-house chips a prominent outside buyer.

November 13

AI helps simulate the Milky Way star by star

November 16

Researchers led by Keiya Hirashima at RIKEN, with the University of Tokyo and Universitat de Barcelona, simulated a model of the Milky Way with more than 100 billion individual stars. A deep-learning model trained on high-resolution supernova simulations predicted how gas spreads after each explosion; simulating one million years took 2.78 hours, against an estimated 315 hours by conventional methods. The work was to be presented at the SC ’25 supercomputing conference, held November 16–21.27

The surrogate let a physics simulation skip its most expensive small-scale calculations.

USPTO revises guidance on AI-assisted inventions

November 26 announcement; November 28 publication

The US Patent and Trademark Office announced revised inventorship guidance, replacing its February 2024 guidance. It reaffirmed that only natural persons can be inventors and that the same inventorship standard applies whether or not AI is used. AI systems were treated as tools assisting human inventors.41,42

The revision applied the ordinary inventorship test to AI-assisted work, withdrawing the separate framework issued in 2024.

Poetic prompts expose a weakness in safety testing

Reported November 30

The Guardian reported research from Icaro Lab, part of the Italian company DexAI, with Sapienza University of Rome and Sant’Anna School of Advanced Studies. Harmful requests written as verse bypassed safeguards in tests across 25 models from nine providers: 20 handcrafted poems averaged a 62 percent success rate, and 1,200 prose prompts converted to verse raised the average from 8 percent to 43 percent. Outputs were judged by three models, with human review.43,44

The study asked whether safeguards survive a change in literary form, and found that style alone could shift a model’s response.
